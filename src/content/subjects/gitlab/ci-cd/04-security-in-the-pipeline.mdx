Every GitLab security feature is, mechanically, **a CI/CD job that emits a JSON
report artifact**. That's the whole trick. There is no separate scanning product
with its own schedule and its own inbox — you `include:` a template, a job runs
in your pipeline, and its report becomes a panel in the merge request.

That framing explains the behaviour people find surprising: if the job doesn't
run in the pipeline, the feature does not exist for that commit.

## The scanners

| Scanner | Finds | Tier |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Secret detection** | Credentials committed to the repo, across the full history | Free |
| **SAST** | Static analysis of your source, language-detected | Free (a subset); full ruleset Ultimate |
| **Dependency scanning** | Known CVEs in your dependencies, plus an **SBOM** | Ultimate |
| **Container scanning** | CVEs in the OS packages of the image you just built | Ultimate |
| **DAST** | Attacks a running instance of your app — often the review app | Ultimate |
| **IaC scanning** | Misconfigured Terraform, Kubernetes manifests, Helm charts | Free |
| **License scanning** | Dependency licences against an allow/deny list | Ultimate |
| **Fuzz testing** | Coverage-guided and API fuzzing | Ultimate |

Note what DAST implies: the pipeline built a review app, then pointed a scanner
at the live URL, per merge request. That's only cheap because environments,
review apps and scanning are the same system.

## Differential findings

The merge request shows **new** findings — the ones your branch introduces
relative to the target branch — not the project's entire backlog. A ten-year-old
codebase has hundreds of findings and adopting a scanner does not require fixing
them first; it requires not *adding* to them. The full set still lives in the
project's Vulnerability Report, where a finding can be confirmed, dismissed with
a reason, or turned into an issue.

## Policies: making it a gate

A scan that produces a report changes nothing on its own. **Security policies**
(Ultimate) are what give it teeth, and they are defined in a separate,
separately-protected policy project — so the developer editing `.gitlab-ci.yml`
cannot remove the scan that guards their own merge.

- **Scan execution policy** — "every project in this group runs secret detection
  and dependency scanning, whether or not their `.gitlab-ci.yml` says so."
- **Scan result / merge request approval policy** — "a new Critical vulnerability
  requires approval from the AppSec group before this MR may merge."
- **Pipeline execution policy** — inject required jobs into every pipeline in
  scope.
- **Compliance frameworks** — label projects (`SOX`, `PCI`) and attach the
  required pipeline and the approval settings to the label, applied across the
  group.

That separation — *the pipeline is owned by the team, the policy is owned by
security, and the policy wins* — is the actual product here. The scanners
themselves are mostly open-source tools you could run yourself.
