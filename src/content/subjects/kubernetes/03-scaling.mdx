The **Horizontal Pod Autoscaler** is a controller like any other: it reads a
metric, computes a desired replica count, and writes it to the Deployment's
`spec.replicas`. The Deployment then does the actual scaling. The whole thing is
one formula:

```text
desiredReplicas = ceil( currentReplicas × ( currentMetric / desiredMetric ) )
```

Two details save you from a thrashing cluster:

1. **The tolerance band.** If the ratio is within 10% of 1.0, the HPA does
   nothing at all. A fleet at 52% CPU against a 50% target is *not* rescaled.
2. **`ceil`.** Scaling up is aggressive by design — half a Pod of demand still
   costs you a whole Pod.

## Try it

Push the observed CPU past the target and watch the ratio cross out of the
tolerance band. Notice how the curve flattens: each extra replica buys less
relief than the one before, because you're dividing a fixed load by more pods.

## Why CPU is a trap

CPU is the default metric because it's always available, not because it's a good
proxy for user pain. A service that spends its time waiting on a database is
slow and idle at the same time — CPU stays flat while latency climbs, and the
HPA never fires. Scale on the thing you actually care about:

- **Requests per second** or **concurrency** for request-driven services.
- **Queue depth** (via an external metric) for workers — this is the one where
  `currentReplicas` in the formula matters most, since a deep queue with zero
  running replicas can't scale from a per-pod metric at all.
- **p99 latency** only as a last resort; it's noisy and lags the cause.

Also: the HPA cannot help if the Pods can't be scheduled. If every node is full,
the HPA writes `replicas: 20`, the ReplicaSet creates 20 Pods, and 8 sit
`Pending` forever — unless a **Cluster Autoscaler** is adding nodes underneath.
