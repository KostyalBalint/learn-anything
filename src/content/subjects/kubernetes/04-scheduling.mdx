The scheduler's whole job is one decision, repeated: given a Pod with no
`spec.nodeName`, pick a node. It runs in two phases.

**Filter** is a hard yes/no per node: does the Pod's CPU/memory request fit in
what's left, does it tolerate the node's taints, does its affinity allow this
node. **Score** ranks the survivors — spread across zones, prefer nodes that
already have the image, prefer nodes that end up least (or most) utilised.

If filtering leaves nothing, the Pod stays `Pending` and the scheduler retries.
It never bends a constraint to make a Pod fit.

## Requests decide placement, limits decide throttling

This is the distinction that costs people the most time:

| Field | Who reads it | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `requests` | The scheduler | Reserved on the node. Placement arithmetic uses this and only this. |
| `limits` | The kubelet / runtime | Enforced at runtime. CPU over limit is throttled; memory over limit is **OOMKilled**. |

A Pod with `requests: 100m` and `limits: 2` will happily be scheduled onto a
crowded node and then get throttled to a crawl. A Pod with `requests: 2` and no
limit is guaranteed 2 cores of room and can burst past it. The node is oversubscribed
whenever the sum of limits exceeds capacity — which is normal and fine, right up
until it isn't.

Memory has no throttling. Exceed a memory limit and the container is killed, not
slowed. That's why `OOMKilled` shows up as a restart loop rather than latency.

## Bin packing, not division

Capacity is not "total CPU ÷ request". Each node fits `floor(allocatable / request)`
Pods and the remainder is **stranded** — free CPU that can never hold another
replica. Push the request up and watch pods-per-node drop in whole steps.

`allocatable` is already less than the node's physical capacity: the kubelet
reserves some for the OS and itself, and DaemonSets take a bite out of every
node before your Deployment sees any of it.

## QoS: the class you get, not the one you pick

You never write `qosClass`. The kubelet derives it from requests and limits, and
uses it to decide who dies first when a node runs out of memory.

| Class | You wrote | Evicted |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `Guaranteed` | limits == requests, on every container | Last |
| `Burstable` | requests set, limits higher or absent | In the middle |
| `BestEffort` | no requests, no limits | First |

So "I left resources unset to keep it simple" means "this Pod is first in line to
be evicted under pressure". Set requests on anything you care about.

## Steering placement

Four mechanisms, and they answer different questions:

- **`nodeSelector`** — the blunt one. Simple label equality, no operators. Fine for `disktype: ssd`.
- **Node affinity** — same idea with expressions, plus a *preferred* mode that only influences scoring instead of filtering.
- **Pod affinity / anti-affinity** — place relative to *other Pods*: keep replicas apart (`topologyKey: kubernetes.io/hostname` spreads across nodes), or keep a cache next to its consumer. Expensive to evaluate; the required form on a large cluster slows scheduling noticeably.
- **Taints and tolerations** — the inverse of everything above. A taint on a node *repels* Pods; only Pods with a matching toleration may land. This is how control-plane and GPU nodes stay clear of general workloads.

The key asymmetry: a toleration does not *attract*. Tolerating the GPU taint
means you're allowed on a GPU node, not that you'll get one — that needs affinity
or a selector too.

`topologySpreadConstraints` is the modern answer to "spread my replicas evenly":
declare a `topologyKey` and a `maxSkew` and let the scheduler balance, rather than
hand-rolling anti-affinity rules.
