Pods are cattle: a rollout replaces every one of them, and each replacement gets
a new IP. So nothing can hold a Pod IP and expect it to keep working. A
**Service** is the stable thing you talk to instead.

The selector is the whole idea: a Service names a **label**, not a set of Pods.
The endpoint controller keeps an EndpointSlice in sync with whatever Pods
currently carry that label *and* pass their readiness probe. Roll out a new
ReplicaSet and the Service follows automatically — you never re-point anything.

Which is also the failure mode. A Service with no matching Pods is not an
error; it's an empty EndpointSlice and a connection that hangs or refuses. When
a Service "doesn't work", check that the selector matches the Pod's labels, and
that the Pods are actually **Ready** — an unready Pod is silently pulled out of
rotation.

## The types, in order of how far they reach

| Type | Reachable from | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `ClusterIP` | Inside the cluster only | The default. A virtual IP, load-balanced across endpoints by kube-proxy. |
| `NodePort` | Any node's IP, on a high port | Mostly a building block; rarely what you want directly. |
| `LoadBalancer` | The internet | Asks the cloud provider for an external LB pointing at the NodePorts. |
| `ExternalName` | — | Not a proxy at all; just a DNS CNAME to something outside the cluster. |

A `LoadBalancer` per service gets expensive fast. That's what an **Ingress** (or
Gateway API) is for: one load balancer, routing by host and path to many
ClusterIP Services behind it.

Note that a ClusterIP is not a real interface anywhere — it's a rule. kube-proxy
programs iptables/IPVS on every node so that packets to that IP get rewritten to
one of the endpoint Pod IPs. Nothing is listening on the ClusterIP itself, which
is why you can't ping it.
